Dispatch 01 Kansas City to Kyushu · 1966–1995 September 2026

Roots, Boots, and the Road to Kyushu

Kansas City, the Corps, and the two years that pointed toward Tokyo

If I had to do it all again, I would still choose to be born in Kansas City, Missouri, in 1966. It was a good place to be a kid — I was never much of an athlete, reliably the last one picked for any team, and my parents divorced early. I grew up mostly in my mother and grandparents’ house, and both grandparents had lived through the Depression as adults. Whatever else that household gave me, it left a set of plain, load-bearing values that never really wore off. So I am grateful to have lived with my maternal grandparents for as long as I did.

What I did have, from very early on, was books. Long before high school I almost always had one in hand — the classics, novels I was probably too young for, whatever was around. To say the least, I wasn’t a very good nor disciplined student in high school. Whatever real education I got in those years, I got it myself, off the shelf.

The road to the Marine Corps was not a straight one, and there is a small joke buried in it: I was thrown out of my first high school by a Marine, of all people, which put the Corps about as far from my plans as a career could get. At the second high school, a recruiter named Sergeant Washington found me anyway and asked whether I’d ever considered it. Something in the question landed. I thought: if I can make it in the Marines, I can do anything in life. My friends and family were, by every account, stunned — I was probably the last person anyone who knew me would have expected to enlist in the Marines — nevertheless, I did enlist, stood on the yellow footprints at Marine Corps Recruit Depot, San Diego, five days after graduating from high school, was a proud USMC PFC ninety days later, and soon thereafter I was in New Orleans as a freshman at Loyola, having already fallen for NOLA on high-school trips to the World’s Fair and Mardi Gras.

If I can make it in the Marines, I can do anything in life.

Loyola turned out to be exactly right — small enough that the professors actually knew who you were. Paying my own way and quite a bit more mature than I had been mere months earlier, I became a serious student. English was the major, for the love of the books; Philosophy went on top of it, mostly to put some discipline into how I thought. To finish both on schedule I carried twenty-one credit hours a semester for my last three semesters, which is not a pace I’d recommend to anyone. Father Ben Wren, one of my professors, inspired me to learn everything I could about Japan, and I chased it well past what the coursework required — the history, the economics, the language.

I was commissioned a Second Lieutenant the same day I graduated from Loyola, in 1989. After the usual round of service schools, my first permanent duty station was Marine Corps Air Station El Toro, where I served as Adjutant for CSSD-14, 1st Force Service Support Group — legal, personnel and general administration for a detachment of about a hundred and sixty Marines and Sailors, before the Gulf War and after it. During the war itself I was task-organized as Adjutant for CSSD-91, which deployed to Saudi Arabia under 1st FSSG’s Direct Support Command and ran an enemy-prisoner-of-war compound at Kibrit, in the northeast of the country, holding somewhere between ten and twenty thousand Iraqi prisoners. My own job there was personnel and administration for the unit, not logistics — though people are usually surprised regardless, that a recruiter once did the paperwork for an operation that size.

I went to Cal State Long Beach at night, both before and after the war, working toward a master’s in Asian Studies. Every course but one was Japan-related — history, culture, religion, art — a fairly deliberate way of keeping the dream of living and working there alive while I was still in uniform. The degree and my discharge landed the same year, 1993, which meant the timing for Japan was finally right. I secured a place on the JET Program and landed in Buzen, a town of about thirty thousand on Kyushu, to teach at the local public high school.

Two years in rural Japan is its own story — the language, the customs, a level of quiet nobody warns you about — but mostly it was runway. It is where I started looking for a way to Tokyo, and it is exactly where the next dispatch picks up.

Dispatch 02 Tokyo · 1995–2000 September 2026

Accidental Recruiter

From rural Japan to founding Job Dragon

In 1995, I was living out in the Japanese countryside, eager to find a path to Tokyo. I wasn’t a technologist by training, but I had always been an early adopter. Back in ’93 and ’94, accessing information in rural Japan meant wrestling with clunky VAX/VMS Unix systems just to download Usenet newsgroups — a slow, expensive, and tedious chore. Then came Netscape Navigator. The moment I finally downloaded it and saw the web render, it was an immediate lightning bolt: this is the future.

I wanted to be in Tokyo, and I wanted to be part of this emerging internet space. I managed an interview with a telecom research non-profit, but without a technical research background, there wasn’t a fit. Eventually, I found my way to East West Consulting, a recruiting firm in Tokyo, naively assuming they might place me with one of their clients. About thirty seconds into sitting across from the recruiter, reality set in. My Japanese was strictly intermediate, and I realized very quickly that I wasn’t candidate material they could command a fee for.

Then came the realization: they don’t know I wasn’t applying for a job here.

Without missing a beat, I internally switched gears mid-conversation and played the rest of the interview as if my sole intention all along had been to join East West as a recruiter myself. To my surprise, I got the offer.

Within a year, I discovered that executive search suited me remarkably well. For the first time in my life, I was making serious money. What was meant to be a stepping stone turned into nearly five years at the firm — years that were not only lucrative, but deeply formative. I loved the team, the thrill of closing placements, and above all, the daily access to seasoned executive leadership. Speaking multiple times a day with senior CEOs and tech leaders was a masterclass in business; I was holding my own and learning rapidly alongside executives much older and more experienced than I was.

Starting my own firm hadn’t even crossed my mind. I was loyal to the founders and assumed I’d stay for the foreseeable future. But everything shifted during a mid-day interview cycle with one of my key clients — a highly successful VC entrepreneur leading his company’s Japan operations. After wrapping up candidate interviews in a hotel lobby, he invited me over to his nearby home for lunch. Over a casual meal, he turned to me and asked: “Mark, why haven’t you started your own recruiting company?”

I told him candidly that I was happy where I was and hadn’t considered it. But as I sat across from a seasoned venture capitalist, the weight of who was asking clicked. I looked at him and said, “Look, if you’re telling me you would evaluate a business plan for a new startup, I’m not asking you to invest right now because I know you’re busy. All I ask is that if I spend the time to write a serious plan, you give it your honest consideration. I’m making great money and I’m happy doing what I’m doing, so don’t waste my time.”

He didn’t hesitate: “Yeah, bring it to me. We’ll look at it.”

That conversation kicked off a six-month sprint. I assembled a small team of co-founders — fellow colleagues from East West — and we set out to write a bulletproof business plan. But we didn’t just write a proposal; we put real skin in the game. Before waiting for any VC money, my co-founders and I self-funded the startup, committing several hundred thousand dollars of our own capital. We even took the risky step of resigning from East West before securing institutional backing.

True to his word, my client evaluated our plan — and he didn’t just review it. His firm signed on as our first strategic core anchor investor. That initial leap of faith is how we launched Job Dragon, the venture that would later evolve into the Ingenium Group.
